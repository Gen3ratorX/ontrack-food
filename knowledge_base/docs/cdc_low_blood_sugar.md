# Low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia) — CDC

Note on units: state mmol/L first (used in Ghana), then mg/dL in brackets. CDC publishes mg/dL. 1 mmol/L = 18 mg/dL, so 3.9 mmol/L is 70 mg/dL and 3.0 mmol/L is 54 mg/dL, matching ADA definitions.

## Key points
- Low blood sugar can be dangerous if left untreated.
- Blood sugar below 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) is considered low.
- Low blood sugar is especially common in people with type 1 diabetes.

## Night-time low blood sugar
Low blood sugar can happen at any time, including during sleep. Reasons include having an active day, being physically active close to bedtime, taking too much insulin, and drinking alcohol at night. Eating regular meals and not skipping them can help avoid night-time lows. Eating when you drink alcohol can also help. If you think you are at risk of low blood sugar overnight, have a snack before bed. A continuous glucose monitor (CGM) can alert you if your blood sugar gets low while you are sleeping.

## Severe low blood sugar
As low blood sugar gets worse, symptoms can include feeling weak, trouble walking or seeing clearly, acting strange or feeling confused, and having seizures. Severe low blood sugar is below 3.0 mmol/L (54 mg/dL). Blood sugar this low may make you faint (pass out). Often you will need someone to help you treat severe low blood sugar.

## Symptoms
Reactions differ between people. Common symptoms include fast heartbeat, shaking, sweating, nervousness or anxiety, irritability or confusion, dizziness, and hunger.

## Hypoglycaemia unawareness
Some people have no symptoms when their blood sugar is low. This makes it harder to treat lows early and increases the risk of severe lows. It is more likely if you have had diabetes for more than 5–10 years, often have low blood sugar, or take certain medicines such as beta blockers. If this applies, check blood sugar more often, especially before driving or being physically active.

## Causes
Taking too much insulin; not eating enough carbohydrate for the insulin taken; the timing of insulin; the amount and timing of physical activity; drinking alcohol; how much fat, protein and fibre are in a meal; hot and humid weather; unexpected changes in schedule; high altitude; puberty; menstruation.
