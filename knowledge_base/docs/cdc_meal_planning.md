# Diabetes meal planning — CDC

A meal plan is a guide for when, what and how much to eat, considering goals, tastes, lifestyle and medicines. A good plan includes more non-starchy vegetables, fewer added sugars and refined grains (such as white bread, rice and pasta), and whole foods instead of highly processed foods where possible.

Carbohydrates raise blood sugar. How fast depends on the food and what you eat with it: fruit juice raises blood sugar faster than whole fruit, and eating carbs with protein, fat or fibre slows the rise. Plan regular, balanced meals; eating about the same amount of carbs at each meal can help.

## Counting carbs
Work with a doctor or registered dietitian to find how many carbs to aim for at each meal.

## The plate method
Start with a 9-inch (about 23 cm) dinner plate:
- Fill half with non-starchy vegetables (for example salad, green beans, garden eggs, okra or leafy greens — local examples added by OnTrack).
- Fill one quarter with lean protein (chicken, beans, tofu, eggs, fish).
- Fill one quarter with carbohydrate foods (grains, starchy vegetables, rice, pasta, beans, fruit, yoghurt; milk also counts).
Drink water or a low-calorie drink.

## Portion size
A portion is what you choose to eat; a serving is a defined amount. Restaurant portions can equal 3–4 servings. CDC "handy" guide: palm (no fingers) ≈ 3 oz meat/fish/poultry; thumb ≈ 1 oz cheese or meat; fist ≈ 1 cup or 1 medium fruit; cupped hand ≈ 1–2 oz nuts; thumb tip ≈ 1 tablespoon; fingertip ≈ 1 teaspoon.

## Support
Ask your doctor for referral to diabetes self-management education and support (DSMES), where a diabetes educator can make a meal plan with you.
