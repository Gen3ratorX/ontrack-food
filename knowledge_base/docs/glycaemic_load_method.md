# Glycaemic load (GL)

- GI ranks how fast the available carbohydrate in a food raises blood glucose. Low GI is 55 or under; high GI is 70 or over (medium 56–69).
- Glycaemic load combines GI with the AMOUNT of carbohydrate eaten: GL = GI ÷ 100 × available carbohydrate (g) in the serving.
- GI News cut-offs per serving: high GL is 20 or above, medium GL 11–19, low GL 10 or under.
- Example from GI News: an apple with GI 38 and 15 g available carbohydrate has GL ≈ 6; eating two apples doubles the GL.

## How OnTrack's meal classifier uses this (project method, not a clinical standard)
- GL is computed only when a food has BOTH a cited GI value (from the Ghanaian GI studies in this knowledge base) AND an available-carbohydrate value from FAO/INFOODS WAFCT 2019. It is always labelled an estimate, because portion sizes are assumptions and the GI studies were small studies in healthy adults.
- For foods with carbohydrate data but no cited GI (e.g., rice, gari, plantain), OnTrack gives a carbohydrate-only rating, using CDC's 15 g carbohydrate serving: up to 30 g (2 servings) low, 31–60 g (3–4 servings) medium, above 60 g high. These carbohydrate cut-offs are an OnTrack heuristic to be validated by a dietitian.
- Protein, fat, fibre and non-starchy vegetables eaten with a starch slow the rise in blood sugar (CDC), so a meal with at least two of these, coming from at least two different foods, is moved down one level. Sugary drinks or added sugar, a large portion (1.5 times the usual or more), or more than 90 g of estimated carbohydrate block this adjustment. GL estimates are rounded to whole numbers before applying the cut-offs.
- Foods with neither a cited GI nor carbohydrate data are reported as insufficient evidence.
